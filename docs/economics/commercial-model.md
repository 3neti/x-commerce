# Commercial Model

## Status

Current status: Phase II commercial architecture draft.

This document is the canonical commercial model for x-commerce economics. It explains how the ecosystem may create, price, allocate, and sustain value without compromising the principles already documented in the foundations, stakeholder business cases, and Commercial Waterfall.

This is not a pricing sheet, forecast, accounting policy, legal opinion, tax analysis, investment offering, contract, or software design.

No prices, revenue shares, royalties, commissions, margins, forecasts, or stakeholder allocations are approved by this document.

## Purpose

This document answers:

```text
How does the ecosystem make money without compromising its principles?
```

It should be read before building:

- the assumptions register;
- five-year projection frameworks;
- stakeholder financial views;
- money-flow diagrams;
- Commercial Waterfall templates;
- legal handoff packages;
- future software primitives.

The guiding principle for Phase II is:

```text
Let the architecture drive the economics.
```

Spreadsheets should not define the commercial model. The commercial model should define the spreadsheets.

## Foundational Thesis

x-commerce begins from these axioms:

```text
The instruction is the transaction.
The transaction is the product.
The product is the business.
```

The customer is purchasing an outcome, not a software feature.

The ecosystem therefore does not primarily sell code, Pay Codes, APIs, notifications, KYC checks, or dashboards. Those may become components of a commercial offering, but the customer buys the completed business outcome:

- payroll completed;
- remittance delivered;
- payout completed;
- collection received;
- merchant payment settled;
- subsidy verified;
- beneficiary notified;
- identity confidence improved;
- feedback collected;
- evidence preserved.

The commercial model exists to explain how those outcomes become sustainable businesses for the participants required to deliver them.

The ecosystem sells packaged outcomes.

The initial RBAP Digital Banking Program may package outcomes such as:

- branded digital transaction access;
- payroll disbursement;
- domestic remittance;
- inward remittance;
- bulk payouts;
- merchant payments;
- collections;
- government benefits and subsidies;
- Pay Code-supported transactions;
- beneficiary validation;
- identity verification;
- transaction notifications;
- feedback;
- surveys;
- post-transaction riders or calls to action;
- reporting;
- audit and evidentiary services;
- managed cloud and operations where separately approved.

These are not all one product. They are modular commercial offerings that a participating institution may adopt incrementally.

The commercial model should support:

- one use case at launch;
- expansion into related use cases;
- optional value-added services;
- different provider arrangements;
- different licensing or subscription structures;
- different Commercial Waterfalls where appropriate;
- future ecosystems beyond RBAP.

## Who Buys

The buyer depends on the outcome.

Potential buyers or sponsors include:

- participating rural banks;
- employers;
- merchants;
- cooperatives;
- NGOs;
- schools;
- churches;
- associations;
- government agencies;
- remitters;
- family sponsors;
- institutional program sponsors;
- public-sector programs;
- future ecosystem operators.

The buyer is the party with the commercial intent to complete an outcome.

The buyer may not be the same as:

- the recipient;
- the rural bank;
- the settlement participant;
- the provider;
- the party paying a pass-through charge;
- the party receiving the direct operational benefit.

Each commercial model must identify the buyer explicitly.

## Who Pays

The payer is the party from whom money is collected.

Possible payers include:

- the rural bank;
- the depositor or customer;
- an employer;
- a merchant;
- a sponsor;
- a government agency;
- a remitter;
- a beneficiary, if legally and commercially approved;
- ODTI, where provider services are bundled;
- another contracting entity.

Buyer and payer may differ. For example:

- an employer buys payroll completion and pays the rural bank or platform charge;
- a government agency sponsors a benefit distribution while recipients pay nothing;
- a rural bank pays a platform subscription while customers pay transaction fees;
- ODTI pays a provider under a wholesale structure while collecting a bundled customer-facing price.

Every model must identify:

- who buys;
- who pays;
- who receives the outcome;
- who receives operational revenue;
- who bears provider costs;
- who bears taxes, rail fees, support costs, and bad debt;
- what happens if the payer does not pay.

## Who Receives Value

Value is broader than revenue.

Different stakeholders may receive different kinds of value:

- customers receive completed outcomes, less administrative work, evidence, recipient flexibility, and operational simplicity;
- recipients may receive freedom, access, dignity, flexibility, and easier receipt of value;
- rural banks receive customer-facing and institutional value;
- RBAP may receive institutional value through member capability development;
- ODTI may receive commercial value through repeatable operating programs;
- 3neti may receive knowledge and IP value through reusable architecture;
- NetBank may receive infrastructure value where regulated banking participation is approved;
- DevOps providers may receive operational value through managed operations;
- value-added providers may receive capability value through outcome attachment;
- business development partners may receive relationship value through approved attribution;
- investors may receive confidence value through ownership or financing rights;
- regulators and the public may receive public value through transparency, accountability, resilience, and inclusion.

Not every stakeholder receives money. Not every valuable outcome creates revenue. Not every revenue line creates sustainable value.

## Who Receives Revenue

Revenue must be identified by stakeholder and by basis.

Potential operational revenue recipients include:

- participating rural banks;
- ODTI;
- 3neti, through approved license consideration or royalties;
- NetBank or another infrastructure partner, where fees are approved;
- DevOps providers;
- value-added service providers;
- business development partners, where approved;
- RBAP, where formal participation economics are approved;
- other approved participants.

Investor return is different from operational revenue. Investors generally participate through equity, dividends, appreciation, repayment, conversion rights, governance rights, or other approved financing instruments. They do not automatically participate in Commercial Waterfalls.

Regulators and the public do not receive ordinary commercial compensation. They may receive Public Value.

## Amounts That Are Not Automatically Revenue

The model must not collapse money movement into revenue.

The following are not automatically revenue:

- gross transaction value;
- customer deposits;
- settlement balances;
- pass-through funds;
- float;
- provider selling price;
- uncollected invoices;
- tax amounts collected on behalf of authorities;
- funds held for settlement;
- funds held for recipients or beneficiaries;
- rail or settlement amounts passed through to another participant.

An amount becomes revenue only under an approved legal, contractual, commercial, and accounting basis.

## Core Commercial Chain

The working commercial chain remains:

```text
3neti
    owns and develops the intellectual property
        |
ODTI
    licenses and commercially operates the technology
        |
Participating rural banks
    obtain licenses through ODTI
        |
Depositors, employers, remitters, merchants, government agencies,
and other customers consume rural-bank-branded digital services
```

This chain preserves several invariants:

- 3neti and ODTI are economically distinct.
- ODTI is a proposed licensee and commercial operator, not the IP owner.
- Rural banks obtain access through ODTI, subject to approved terms.
- Customers consume outcomes through rural-bank-branded services.
- NetBank, DevOps providers, value-added providers, business development partners, investors, RBAP, and other participants participate only through approved structures.

The commercial model must never hide this chain inside one blended revenue story.

## Operating Revenue Versus Ownership Return

Operating revenue arises from commercial activity:

- licenses;
- subscriptions;
- transactions;
- provider services;
- managed operations;
- implementation;
- maintenance;
- approved markups;
- approved Commercial Waterfalls.

Ownership return arises from ownership or financing rights:

- equity appreciation;
- dividends;
- repayment;
- conversion;
- approved investor return;
- governance rights;
- strategic rights.

These mechanisms must not be confused.

Operational participants may receive allocations through Commercial Waterfalls. Investors generally participate through approved ownership or financing instruments. A person or entity could theoretically be both an operational participant and an investor, but the rights must be documented separately.

## Recurring Relationships Versus Recurring Revenue

Recurring revenue matters, but recurring commercial relationships matter more.

Revenue follows relationships when:

- customers repeat outcomes;
- rural banks expand from one module to another;
- ODTI operates repeatable programs;
- 3neti converts commercial learning into reusable architecture;
- providers attach useful capabilities;
- DevOps operations remain reliable;
- partners maintain trusted relationships;
- public confidence grows.

The long-term asset is not merely a subscription line. It is the accumulated trust and repeatability that allow Commercial Events to continue occurring.

This may later mature into a concept such as Relationship Capital, but this document treats it as an architectural observation rather than formal grammar.

## Modular Offerings

The commercial model should support modular adoption.

A participating institution should be able to adopt:

- payroll only;
- remittance only;
- government payouts only;
- merchant payments only;
- collections only;
- Pay Codes only;
- notifications only;
- KYC-attached payouts only;
- reporting only;
- managed operations only where appropriate.

Modular adoption matters because different banks and customers have different local demand, readiness, risk tolerance, support capacity, and legal requirements.

The business should grow by proving one outcome, then expanding responsibly.

## Pricing Model Layers

The pricing model may include several layers.

### Access Layer

Examples:

- license;
- subscription;
- maintenance;
- activation;
- onboarding.

Purpose: compensate access, platform availability, commercial enablement, support scope, and IP licensing where approved.

### Implementation Layer

Examples:

- setup;
- integration;
- training;
- migration;
- configuration;
- launch support.

Purpose: compensate human work required to make the offering usable.

### Transaction Layer

Examples:

- base transaction fee;
- per-disbursement fee;
- per-remittance fee;
- per-collection fee;
- per-successful-outcome fee;
- per-qualifying-event fee.

Purpose: align price with completed or qualifying transaction outcomes.

### Capability Layer

Examples:

- SMS;
- email;
- KYC;
- OTP;
- validation;
- identity;
- feedback;
- rider;
- reporting;
- archival evidence;
- reconciliation.

Purpose: attach specialized capabilities that improve outcome completion, evidence, reach, compliance, communication, or usefulness.

### Operations Layer

Examples:

- managed cloud setup;
- recurring managed operations;
- monitoring;
- backups;
- incident response;
- release support;
- certificate maintenance;
- disaster-recovery exercises.

Purpose: compensate operational responsibility without transferring ownership of production assets unless a future approved model says otherwise.

### Participation Layer

Examples:

- commission;
- referral fee;
- success fee;
- revenue share;
- royalty;
- institutional participation;
- marketplace participation.

Purpose: allocate approved commercial value to participants with valid Commercial Rights and attribution.

## Provider Costs And Markups

Provider cost must remain visible.

For value-added services, managed operations, rail usage, settlement, identity, messaging, reporting, or other third-party services, every commercial model should distinguish:

- customer-facing price;
- direct provider cost;
- pass-through amount;
- gross markup;
- gross margin;
- taxes;
- ODTI retained amount;
- bank-retained amount;
- 3neti royalty basis, if any;
- partner allocation, if any;
- net platform revenue.

Do not treat the full selling price of SMS, email, KYC, riders, identity, DevOps, rail, or other provider services as ODTI revenue unless ODTI is legally and contractually the revenue recipient and the provider cost is accounted for separately.

Provider economics should be explicit enough that a stakeholder can answer:

```text
What part of this price pays the provider, and what part is margin or allocation?
```

NetBank or another infrastructure participant may receive economics through:

- approved settlement fees;

- account or infrastructure services;
- other approved infrastructure participation.

The model must preserve:

- settlement balances are not revenue;
- deposits are regulated concepts;
- float must not be assumed as owned revenue;
- rail and settlement charges must be separated;
- regulated role characterization belongs to x-legal.

Infrastructure value is not the same as gross transaction value.

## DevOps And Managed Operations Economics

Managed operations may be structured in several ways:

1. Rural bank contracts directly with the DevOps provider.
2. ODTI contracts with the DevOps provider and resells managed operations.
3. ODTI bundles managed hosting into a platform subscription and pays the provider under a wholesale arrangement.

Each model should distinguish:

- who owns the production environment;
- who delegates authority;
- who operates;
- who pays cloud bills;
- who pays managed operations fees;
- who bears margin or support risk;
- who handles incidents;
- who can replace the provider.

The central invariant remains:

```text
The DevOps provider operates.
The rural bank owns.
```

Unless a future approved model explicitly changes ownership, the rural bank should own its production environment, domains, DNS, certificates, databases, backups, credentials, and cloud billing relationship.

## Partner Participation Economics

Business development partners, referral partners, institutional participants, or future channel partners may participate only through approved commercial models.

Partner economics should define:

- qualifying Commercial Event;
- attribution basis;
- Commercial Right, if any;
- allocation base;
- allocation rule;
- duration;
- caps or exclusions;
- deduction order;
- reversal or refund treatment;
- payment timing;
- approval authority;
- conflict resolution.

Partner allocations must not arise from discretion, customer funds, deposits, settlement balances, or vague involvement.

The governing principle is:

```text
Compensation should be earned by commercial events, not by discretion.
```

Channel Partner remains deferred until a genuine channel-distribution model emerges.

## Investor Economics

Investor economics are ownership or financing economics, not ordinary operational allocations.

Investors may participate through:

- equity;
- dividends;
- appreciation;
- repayment;
- conversion rights;
- governance rights;
- information rights;
- strategic rights.

Investors do not automatically participate in Commercial Waterfalls.

The investor thesis is:

```text
Investors are not funding software.
They are funding the creation of repeatable commercial relationships.
```

Any investor return must be modeled only after the investment instrument, ownership rights, repayment terms, dividend policy, and legal requirements are known.

## Regulator And Public-Interest Value

The public-interest model is not a revenue model.

The ecosystem may create Public Value through:

- stronger rural banks;
- financial inclusion;
- transparency;
- accountability;
- public confidence;
- provider replaceability;
- customer freedom;
- operational resilience;
- traceable money flows;
- clearer legal handoffs;
- better audit evidence;
- Commercial Waterfall transparency.

The ecosystem is designed with banks, not around banks.

Commercial architecture should make responsible institutions stronger, not less relevant.

Legal characterization belongs to x-legal.

## Commercial Waterfall Applicability

Commercial Waterfalls apply when approved commercial value must be allocated among participants according to explicit rules.

They may apply to:

- transaction-based partner allocations;
- royalties;
- marketplace participation;
- value-added service markup allocation;
- institutional participation;
- recurring overrides;
- managed operations resale margin;
- other approved participation models.

They do not automatically apply to:

- every Commercial Event;
- every Billable Event;
- customer funds;
- deposits;
- settlement balances;
- pass-through funds;
- investor returns;
- public-interest value;
- provider costs that are simply paid under contract.

A Commercial Waterfall should be used only when the model needs an ordered allocation of approved commercial value.

## Calculation Order For Future Models

Future spreadsheets should follow the commercial model, not invent local logic.

Recommended calculation order:

1. Identify the outcome.
2. Identify buyer, payer, recipient, and sponsoring party.
3. Identify participating stakeholders.
4. Identify Commercial Event.
5. Identify Billable Event, if any.
6. Identify customer-facing price.
7. Separate gross transaction value from fees and revenue.
8. Separate pass-through funds.
9. Deduct taxes where applicable.
10. Deduct rail, settlement, or infrastructure charges.
11. Deduct direct provider costs.
12. Deduct managed operations or cloud costs where applicable.
13. Determine gross markup and margin.
14. Determine bank-retained revenue.
15. Determine ODTI net platform revenue.
16. Determine 3neti royalty or license basis, if any.
17. Determine approved partner allocations, if any.
18. Determine operating costs.
19. Determine net contribution by stakeholder.
20. Report non-financial value indicators where relevant.

The assumptions register should assign identifiers to every input used in this sequence.

## Commercial Governance Rules

The commercial model should follow these rules:

1. No price without an assumption, quote, proposal, contract, or approved pricing decision.
2. No allocation without an approved commercial model.
3. No entitlement without a qualifying event and valid attribution.
4. No use of customer funds, deposits, settlement balances, pass-through amounts, or gross transaction value as distributable revenue unless expressly lawful and approved.
5. Provider costs must remain visible.
6. ODTI and 3neti economics must remain distinct.
7. Investor return must remain distinct from operational Commercial Waterfalls.
8. Public Value must not be reduced to company revenue.
9. Legal characterization belongs to x-legal.
10. Execution behavior belongs to x-change.
11. Deployable customer-product behavior belongs to applications such as x-Payout.
12. Financial projections are not promises.
13. Documentation must remain faithful to actual operations.
