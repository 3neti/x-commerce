# Depositor And Customer

## Stakeholder

A depositor or customer is the person, business, institution, sponsor, employer, merchant, cooperative, NGO, church, school, association, government agency, family, or program sponsor that funds or requests a transaction outcome through a participating rural bank.

This stakeholder should not be understood only as an ordinary retail depositor. In many use cases, the commercially important customer is an organization trying to accomplish work:

- complete payroll;
- distribute petty cash;
- deliver ayuda;
- release scholarships;
- pay employee incentives;
- distribute commissions;
- provide emergency assistance;
- remit family support;
- pay suppliers;
- reach beneficiaries.

This document is a stakeholder business-case draft. It is not evidence that any customer, sponsor, agency, employer, merchant, cooperative, NGO, school, church, family, or institution has accepted the program, approved fees, or entered into a contractual arrangement.

## What's In It For Them?

The program is intended to help depositors and customers accomplish business or personal outcomes with less administrative burden.

The customer is not purchasing software, Pay Codes, APIs, SMS, KYC, riders, dashboards, or bank integrations as ends in themselves. The customer is purchasing a completed outcome. Everything else exists to increase the probability that the desired outcome is completed successfully, evidenced clearly, and repeatable when needed.

The strongest commercial idea is practical relief:

```text
The customer is purchasing an outcome, not a software feature.
```

For many customers, the greatest pain is not sending money. The greatest pain is everything around the act of sending money: collecting recipient details, asking which bank or wallet a recipient uses, correcting account information, validating recipients, following up, answering questions, reconciling results, and proving that the intended recipient was reached.

The program is designed to reduce that surrounding work.

Potential benefits include:

- less administrative work;
- easier payroll;
- easier incentives;
- easier petty cash;
- easier reimbursement;
- easier remittance;
- easier subsidy distribution;
- broader recipient reach;
- fewer onboarding problems;
- recipient flexibility;
- transaction visibility;
- delivery evidence;
- reusable payment workflows;
- reduced follow-up.

No completion rate, cost reduction, recipient satisfaction, service availability, or legal outcome is guaranteed.

## Problem Today

A depositor often spends more effort preparing, coordinating, explaining, and reconciling a payment than sending it.

Common pain points include:

- maintaining spreadsheets;
- collecting account numbers;
- asking recipients which bank, wallet, branch, or provider they use;
- onboarding recipients into a particular platform;
- handling incomplete KYC;
- correcting account information;
- recipients being forced to create accounts;
- recipients forgetting credentials;
- coordinating pickup;
- explaining payment instructions;
- following up whether money was received;
- reconciling reports;
- handling failed transfers;
- changing payment channels;
- redoing payments;
- answering recipient questions;
- lacking proof of completion;
- lacking feedback from recipients.

The payment itself may take seconds. The surrounding work can take days.

```text
Payments take seconds. Work takes days.
```

The depositor is buying relief from administrative burden, not merely another payment channel.

## Why They Would Participate

A depositor or customer participates in the business case to evaluate whether the program can help them accomplish recurring outcomes more reliably, with less coordination work and better evidence.

Potential reasons include:

- complete payroll without forcing every worker into one account or wallet path;
- distribute incentives, commissions, reimbursements, or petty cash with less manual coordination;
- deliver aid, subsidies, scholarships, or emergency support to recipients with fewer logistical barriers;
- pay suppliers, merchants, field workers, members, beneficiaries, or relatives more consistently;
- provide recipients with more freedom in how they receive value, subject to the approved execution model;
- reduce repeated data collection and recipient support;
- create reusable workflows for recurring distributions;
- obtain reporting and evidence for internal control, donor reporting, program governance, or reconciliation;
- attach value-added services such as notification, validation, KYC, feedback, surveys, or post-transaction calls to action where approved.

These are commercial hypotheses. They must be validated through adoption evidence, fee approval, legal review, recipient experience, operating performance, and actual reduction in administrative work.

## Contribution

The depositor or customer contributes:

- funding;
- business intent;
- recipient information;
- transaction instructions;
- internal approval;
- use-case definition;
- customer-facing or recipient-facing communication where applicable;
- commercial event creation;
- feedback on completion, failures, and administrative effort.

Without this stakeholder, the ecosystem has no customer outcome to deliver. The depositor creates demand for completed outcomes and supplies the commercial reason a transaction should occur.

The customer should not be expected to understand or operate settlement, DevOps, software licensing, commercial architecture, or legal characterization merely because they fund a transaction outcome.

## Receives

Depending on the approved model, the program is intended to provide the depositor or customer with:

- completed outcomes;
- operational simplicity;
- recipient flexibility;
- reusable distribution workflows;
- transaction visibility;
- delivery evidence;
- reporting;
- reconciliation support;
- optional value-added services;
- scalable distribution capability;
- clearer recipient communication;
- a path to expand from one use case to related use cases.

The customer receives business capability, not a guarantee that every recipient, channel, provider, value-added service, or use case will be available at launch.

## Competitive Position

If the program succeeds, the depositor's organization may become easier to work with, easier to pay from, easier to receive support from, or easier to administer.

The intended positioning benefits vary by customer type:

- an employer can make payroll, incentives, reimbursements, and field payments less burdensome;
- an NGO can distribute aid with better evidence and less recipient coordination;
- a cooperative can reach members more effectively;
- a merchant can pay suppliers or field partners more consistently;
- a school can distribute scholarships or allowances more efficiently;
- a church or association can support members with less manual administration;
- a government agency or sponsor can reach beneficiaries with clearer reporting;
- a family can support relatives with fewer logistical barriers.

This is operational positioning, not a claim of market leadership. The customer's actual benefit depends on recipient readiness, fee model, completion rates, support quality, use-case fit, and the ability to reduce work around the transaction.

## Revenue Or Value Drivers

Unlike ODTI, 3neti, providers, or partners, the depositor may not earn direct revenue from the program.

The customer value is primarily operational:

- reduced administrative cost;
- lower payment preparation time;
- fewer failed payments;
- better recipient satisfaction;
- improved employee, member, beneficiary, supplier, or family experience;
- faster distribution;
- stronger program credibility;
- lower reconciliation effort;
- broader reach;
- more repeatable internal workflows;
- better evidence for audit, donor reporting, internal approval, or management review.

Not every stakeholder creates revenue. Some create efficiency, trust, completion, or evidence. For depositors, those forms of value may matter more than direct income.

Commercial perspective:

```text
People do not wake up wanting to send money.
They wake up wanting to accomplish something.
```

The money enables the outcome. In x-commerce terms, the depositor creates Customer Value. Without Customer Value, there is no Commercial Event. Without Commercial Events, there is no Commercial Waterfall. Without a sustainable allocation model, the ecosystem cannot reliably support the participants required to deliver the outcome.

## Costs

Potential depositor or customer costs include:

- transaction fees;
- value-added service fees;
- KYC costs where applicable;
- notification, validation, rider, feedback, or reporting charges where approved;
- payroll or distribution preparation;
- internal approval time;
- recipient data cleanup;
- recipient communication;
- reconciliation work;
- failed-transaction handling;
- support coordination;
- training or process redesign.

The model should separate:

- transaction cost;
- administrative cost;
- provider cost;
- bank or platform fees;
- pass-through funds;
- value-added service charges;
- internal labor cost.

The administrative cost may be larger than the transaction fee. A low transaction fee does not automatically mean the overall process is cheap if the surrounding work remains manual.

## Risks

Key risks for the depositor or customer include:

- poor recipient information;
- incorrect approvals;
- unrealistic expectations about completion timing or recipient options;
- incomplete KYC;
- misunderstood legal or regulatory obligations;
- misunderstanding recipient flexibility;
- reliance on undocumented processes;
- recipient confusion;
- failed or delayed transactions;
- unclear support ownership;
- insufficient internal controls;
- weak reconciliation processes;
- privacy or data-sharing concerns;
- assuming that Pay Codes, notifications, KYC, riders, or other services are automatically available for every use case.

The customer should not treat the program as a substitute for internal approval, recipient authorization, compliance obligations, or clear communication.

## Five-Year Outcome

The five-year outcome for a depositor or customer should emphasize operational maturity, reduced administrative burden, repeatability, and satisfaction.

Possible five-year outcomes include:

- payment and distribution workflows become more repeatable;
- payroll, aid, incentives, remittances, reimbursements, or supplier payments require less manual preparation;
- recipients experience fewer barriers to receiving value;
- internal teams spend less time correcting data, answering status questions, and reconciling exceptions;
- evidence and reporting become easier to produce;
- the customer adds additional use cases after a successful first workflow;
- employee, member, beneficiary, supplier, donor, or family satisfaction improves;
- the organization becomes more confident using rural-bank-branded digital transaction services.

Financial ROI is only part of the story. A useful model should also estimate:

- time saved;
- support requests avoided;
- failed transactions reduced;
- reconciliation effort reduced;
- repeat distributions completed;
- recipient satisfaction;
- process reliability;
- value-added service usefulness.

Required scenarios:

- Conservative: limited repeat use, high recipient-data cleanup, modest reduction in administrative work.
- Base: repeatable distribution workflow, manageable support load, measurable reduction in follow-up and reconciliation.
- Accelerated: multiple use cases adopted, stronger recipient satisfaction, lower manual effort, higher completion reliability.

## If They Do Nothing

If the depositor or customer does nothing, they may avoid change, onboarding, new fees, process redesign, and internal retraining.

The tradeoff is that the existing administrative burden continues:

- manual spreadsheets;
- fragmented payment channels;
- repeated recipient coordination;
- account and wallet collection;
- failed-transfer handling;
- duplicated work;
- manual follow-up;
- unclear proof of receipt;
- recipient complaints;
- slow reconciliation;
- recurring dependence on informal workarounds.

```text
Payments take seconds. Work takes days.
```

The "do nothing" case may still be rational if transaction volume is low, recipient needs are simple, fees are not justified, legal readiness is unresolved, or internal process discipline is not yet ready. But the cost of doing nothing should include administrative work, not only transaction fees avoided.

## Open Decisions

- Which depositor or customer segment should be modeled first: employer, merchant, NGO, school, cooperative, agency, sponsor, family, or another group?
- Which outcome should be the first customer-facing use case?
- Which fees are customer-facing?
- Which fees are sponsor-facing?
- Which fees are absorbed by the rural bank, ODTI, sponsor, or program?
- Which recipient choices are available under the approved execution model?
- Which KYC, validation, notification, feedback, rider, or reporting services are available at launch?
- What evidence proves completion?
- What support channel does the customer use when recipients report issues?
- What recipient data is required?
- What privacy, consent, and disclosure language is required?
- Which customer responsibilities must be included in onboarding?
- How is administrative burden measured before and after adoption?

## Validation Evidence

Evidence that would validate the depositor or customer business case includes:

- completed pilot distribution;
- measured reduction in preparation time;
- measured reduction in follow-up;
- measured reduction in reconciliation effort;
- recipient satisfaction feedback;
- repeat usage by the same customer;
- expansion into a second use case;
- fewer failed transactions or fewer unresolved exceptions;
- support-ticket volume and resolution time;
- customer willingness to pay for the completed outcome;
- customer willingness to pay for selected value-added services;
- internal customer approval to continue or expand the workflow;
- evidence that recipient flexibility reduced onboarding friction.

Validation should focus on whether the program reduces work and improves completion, not merely whether a payment can be sent.
