# Value-Added Service Provider

## Stakeholder

A value-added service provider is a third-party or future internal provider that supplies specialized services which may be attached to transaction outcomes in the RBAP Digital Banking Program or future x-commerce ecosystems.

Possible services include:

- SMS notification;
- email notification;
- KYC;
- OTP;
- digital signature;
- selfie capture;
- face match;
- location verification;
- beneficiary validation;
- feedback;
- surveys;
- document collection;
- fraud or risk analysis;
- reporting;
- archival evidence;
- reconciliation;
- premium support;
- rider URL or post-transaction call to action.

This document is a stakeholder business-case draft. It is not evidence that any provider has been selected, approved, integrated, contracted, certified, priced, or given exclusivity.

The value-added service provider is not the software owner, IP owner, commercial operator, rural bank, settlement institution, DevOps provider, business development partner, legal authority, or automatic owner of customer relationships. Its role is to make a transaction outcome more complete, verifiable, useful, compliant, reachable, or valuable.

## What's In It For Them?

The program is intended to give value-added service providers access to repeatable transaction contexts where their services can improve outcome completion, evidence, communication, identity confidence, customer engagement, or operational control.

The provider is not merely selling an API call. It is contributing a specific capability that may help a customer accomplish work:

- a recipient is notified;
- a beneficiary is validated;
- an identity check is completed;
- a customer receives proof;
- a sponsor receives feedback;
- a document is collected;
- a post-transaction call to action is delivered;
- a transaction becomes easier to reconcile or evidence.

The strongest commercial idea is attachment to outcomes. A value-added service becomes more valuable when it is connected to a successful transaction journey rather than sold as an isolated component.

No transaction volume, attachment rate, provider revenue, exclusivity, integration priority, or continuing participation is guaranteed.

## Problem Today

Specialized providers often sell valuable services, but their services may remain disconnected from the customer outcome that justifies them.

Without a program architecture, providers may face:

- one-off integrations;
- unclear transaction context;
- inconsistent usage volumes;
- manual customer acquisition;
- uncertain pricing authority;
- unclear provider-cost treatment;
- disputes over markup and margin;
- privacy or compliance uncertainty;
- unclear support boundaries;
- limited visibility into whether their service improved the outcome;
- customer confusion about whether the service is required, optional, bundled, or pass-through.

The ecosystem also faces a corresponding risk: value-added services can be misrepresented as pure platform revenue if provider cost, markup, taxes, and stakeholder allocations are not separated.

The program is designed to make provider participation explicit, priced, traceable, and connected to completed outcomes.

## Why They Would Participate

A value-added service provider participates in the business case to evaluate whether the ecosystem can become a repeatable distribution channel for useful transaction-attached services.

Potential reasons include:

- recurring usage connected to transaction outcomes;
- access to participating rural banks and their customers through approved commercial models;
- attachment to payroll, remittance, disbursement, collection, merchant, government, or Pay Code workflows;
- clearer provider-cost and markup treatment;
- better commercial visibility than one-off API resale;
- opportunity to prove that the service improves completion, compliance, communication, evidence, or customer experience;
- possible marketplace or service-menu participation in future phases;
- repeatable integration and support model across multiple institutions;
- evidence that the service creates measurable operational value.

These are commercial hypotheses. They must be validated through provider quotes, approved pricing, legal review, support capacity, data protection, service-level evidence, customer adoption, and actual attachment rates.

## Contribution

The program may ask a value-added service provider to contribute:

- specialized service capability;
- API, portal, batch, manual, or operational service access where approved;
- provider pricing;
- service-level commitments;
- integration documentation;
- data protection and privacy controls;
- support escalation path;
- reporting;
- billing records;
- error handling;
- compliance evidence where applicable;
- provider-side operations and account management;
- feedback on usage, failures, and improvement opportunities.

The provider should not be expected to perform undefined banking, settlement, customer-service, DevOps, legal, or commercial-operator responsibilities merely because its service is attached to a transaction.

## Receives

Depending on approved arrangements, the program is intended to provide a value-added service provider with:

- defined service role;
- approved provider fee or cost basis;
- possible recurring usage;
- distribution through rural-bank transaction workflows;
- clearer integration pathway;
- reporting on qualifying usage;
- opportunity to improve transaction outcomes;
- potential future marketplace or service-menu participation;
- support boundaries that distinguish provider failures from platform, bank, settlement, or customer issues.

The provider receives a possible commercial channel, not a guarantee of volume, margin, customer ownership, exclusivity, or continuing selection.

## Competitive Position

If the program succeeds, the value-added service provider improves its strategic position as a trusted outcome-support provider rather than a commodity API vendor.

The intended positioning benefits include:

- repeatable transaction-attached distribution;
- clearer proof that the service improves completion, reach, evidence, compliance, or customer experience;
- standardized integration expectations;
- stronger credibility with banks, sponsors, merchants, employers, agencies, and operators;
- opportunity to participate in multiple future ecosystems;
- ability to specialize in one high-value capability while the ecosystem handles broader commercial orchestration.

This is strategic positioning, not a claim of preferred-provider status, exclusivity, or guaranteed adoption. The provider's actual position depends on service quality, pricing, reliability, legal readiness, support performance, customer value, and operational fit.

## Revenue Or Value Drivers

Possible value-added service provider revenue or value drivers include:

- usage-based provider fees;
- per-notification charges;
- per-validation charges;
- per-KYC charges;
- per-OTP charges;
- per-document or evidence item charges;
- per-feedback or survey response charges;
- reporting or archive fees;
- fraud, risk, or verification services;
- premium support services;
- recurring service bundles;
- future marketplace participation where approved.

Illustrative working assumptions currently recorded elsewhere in x-commerce include:

```text
SMS notification:       approximately PHP 1.00
Email notification:     approximately PHP 0.10
KYC:                    approximately PHP 25.00
Rider URL / CTA:        approximately PHP 50.00
```

These are working assumptions only. They are not approved prices, provider quotes, customer-facing prices, margins, commissions, or contracts.

The rider URL or CTA amount is especially unresolved. Its billable unit may eventually be per transaction, per campaign, per placement, per response, or another approved commercial model.

The model must separate:

- customer-facing price;
- direct provider cost;
- provider revenue;
- gross markup;
- gross margin;
- taxes;
- settlement or rail charges;
- bank-retained revenue;
- ODTI platform revenue;
- 3neti royalties where applicable;
- partner allocations where approved;
- net platform revenue.

Do not treat the full selling price of SMS, email, KYC, rider, validation, identity, reporting, or other third-party services as ODTI revenue. Provider cost and provider revenue must remain visible.

## Success Indicators

Early indicators that the value-added service provider model is working may include:

- successful integration or operational onboarding;
- clear provider pricing and cost basis;
- successful first attached transaction;
- stable attachment rate by use case;
- service success rate;
- low provider-side failure rate;
- faster recipient completion;
- better notification delivery;
- lower support burden;
- improved validation or identity confidence;
- better reconciliation or evidence quality;
- customer willingness to pay for the service;
- repeat use by the same bank, sponsor, or customer;
- expansion of the service into additional workflows;
- low dispute rate over provider charges;
- clear separation of provider cost and platform markup.

Revenue is important for the provider, but it is not the only signal. The strongest signal is that the service improves the probability, quality, evidence, or usefulness of the completed outcome.

## Costs

Potential value-added service provider costs include:

- service delivery;
- infrastructure;
- API maintenance;
- support staff;
- account management;
- compliance work;
- data protection controls;
- uptime monitoring;
- security review;
- reporting;
- documentation;
- onboarding and integration support;
- failed-event handling;
- billing reconciliation;
- legal review;
- product maintenance;
- customer or partner education.

The ecosystem should distinguish provider costs from platform costs, bank costs, customer-facing prices, taxes, pass-through funds, and stakeholder allocations.

If ODTI or another participant resells a value-added service, the provider's direct charge should not disappear inside a gross price. The economic model should show what is provider cost, what is markup, and what is retained or shared by other participants.

## Risks

Key risks for value-added service providers include:

- low attachment rates;
- unclear billable unit;
- unclear provider-cost treatment;
- integration failure;
- service outages;
- privacy or data-protection issues;
- regulatory or consent gaps;
- pricing pressure;
- support-boundary disputes;
- provider performance being blamed on the rural bank, ODTI, or another participant;
- customer-facing price exceeding perceived value;
- value-added services being bundled without clear cost recovery;
- provider fees being confused with ODTI platform revenue;
- transaction failures caused by provider dependencies;
- unclear reversal or refund treatment;
- vendor lock-in concerns;
- unsupported customer expectations.

The provider should not rely on optimistic attachment rates without use-case evidence and scenario modeling.

## Responsibilities

The value-added service provider may own or share responsibility for:

- delivering the approved service;
- maintaining service availability within agreed boundaries;
- publishing technical or operational documentation;
- protecting data handled by the service;
- providing logs, reports, or evidence where required;
- supporting integration and testing;
- identifying service failures;
- communicating incidents;
- maintaining pricing or quote validity;
- supporting billing reconciliation;
- complying with applicable contractual, privacy, security, and regulatory obligations.

The provider should not automatically own:

- customer contracts;
- platform pricing;
- rural-bank customer support;
- commercial approvals;
- settlement;
- x-change execution semantics;
- DevOps operations;
- legal characterization;
- customer deposits or pass-through funds;
- the overall transaction outcome.

Responsibilities should be documented in provider agreements, integration notes, support boundaries, service-level models, privacy documentation, and commercial-model records.

- approved markup or margin model;
- legal and privacy review;
- data-sharing rules;
- integration approach;
- service-level expectations;
- support escalation path;
- failure and reversal treatment;
- reporting requirements;
- billing and reconciliation process;
- transaction workflow fit;
- customer willingness to pay;
- value-added service attachment rate;

The provider's success depends on being attached to real customer outcomes, not merely being listed as an available service.

## Five-Year Outcome

The five-year model should show provider economics separately from ODTI, rural-bank, 3neti, partner, and customer economics.

Minimum provider-side inputs:

- selected services;
- provider cost or provider price per unit;
- billable unit;
- expected attachment rate by use case;
- transaction volume by use case;
- service success rate;
- support cost per service event;
- integration cost;
- account management cost;
- compliance or reporting cost;
- failed-event, reversal, or refund treatment;
- price changes;
- churn;
- taxes and contingency.

Required scenarios:

- Conservative: low attachment rate, limited use cases, higher support cost, slower provider onboarding.
- Base: practical attachment rate, stable first services, manageable support, visible outcome improvement.
- Accelerated: multiple services attached across several workflows, strong repeat usage, operationally stable integration.

The model should show:

- provider revenue;
- direct provider cost;
- gross margin retained by provider;
- customer-facing price;
- ODTI markup or service margin, if any;
- bank-retained amount, if any;
- partner allocation, if approved;
- tax treatment;
- impact on customer outcome completion.

The provider's five-year outcome should not be represented as a share of gross transaction value, deposits, settlement balances, or pass-through funds.

## If They Do Nothing

If the value-added service provider does nothing, it may avoid integration cost, support obligations, banking-related diligence, data-sharing review, and operational complexity.

The tradeoff is that the provider may remain outside transaction workflows where its service could create higher value. It may continue selling one-off services without recurring transaction context, standardized integration, or evidence that its service improves completed outcomes.

The ecosystem also loses optional capabilities that may improve completion, trust, reach, evidence, compliance, communication, and customer satisfaction.

The "do nothing" case may still be rational if the provider cannot meet reliability, privacy, support, price, or legal-readiness expectations.

## Open Decisions

- Which value-added services should be offered first?
- Which services are first-party, third-party, or future marketplace services?
- Which provider quotes support the working assumptions?
- Which fees are customer-facing?
- Which fees are sponsor-facing?
- Which fees are paid by the rural bank, ODTI, customer, sponsor, merchant, employer, agency, or another participant?
- Which fees are pass-through provider costs?
- Which markup or margin is approved?
- Which services require legal, privacy, security, or regulatory review?
- What is the billable unit for each service?
- What happens when the underlying transaction fails?
- What happens when the value-added service fails but the transaction succeeds?
- What happens when the value-added service succeeds but the transaction fails?
- Which provider owns support for service-specific failures?
- Which data may be shared with the provider?
- Which reports or evidence must the provider return?
- Can multiple providers offer substitute services?
- Should ODTI certify approved providers or approved service standards?
