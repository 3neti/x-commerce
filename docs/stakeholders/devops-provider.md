# DevOps Provider

## Stakeholder

The DevOps provider is an independent managed operations participant that may deploy, operate, monitor, maintain, and support production environments for participating rural banks under an approved commercial model.

This document is a stakeholder business-case draft. It is not evidence that any DevOps provider has accepted a role, approved pricing, agreed to service levels, received bank authorization, or become an exclusive provider.

The DevOps provider is not the software owner, IP owner, commercial operator, bank, settlement institution, or legal authority. Its role is to enable reliable production operation.

## What's In It For Them?

The program is intended to create repeatable managed operations opportunities for qualified DevOps providers.

The DevOps provider is selling operational excellence, not software. Its value is the ability to provision, deploy, monitor, secure, back up, restore, patch, document, and support production environments reliably enough for a rural-bank digital transaction program.

The strongest commercial idea is that each participating bank should remain the owner of its production environment while the DevOps provider operates that environment under delegated authority. This preserves bank control, supports provider replaceability, and builds trust in the ecosystem.

No DevOps provider revenue, recurring engagement, service level, exclusivity, customer retention, or provider certification is guaranteed.

## Problem Today

Participating rural banks may need production-grade operations but may not have internal DevOps capacity, cloud operations experience, monitoring discipline, backup processes, incident-response procedures, or release support.

At the same time, unclear operating models can create serious commercial and trust problems:

- the bank may not know who owns production assets;
- ODTI may be blamed for infrastructure issues outside its control;
- the DevOps provider may carry undefined support obligations;
- production credentials may be handled informally;
- backups may exist without restoration verification;
- cloud costs may grow without monitoring;
- incident response may lack clear escalation paths;
- provider replacement may become difficult if ownership boundaries are unclear.

The program is designed to separate ownership from operation. The participating rural bank owns the production environment. The DevOps provider operates and maintains it under approved authority.

## Receives

Depending on approved arrangements, the program is intended to provide the DevOps provider with:

- deployment setup opportunities;
- recurring managed operations revenue;
- repeatable operating standards;
- a pipeline of rural-bank deployments, if adoption occurs;
- clearer support boundaries;
- ecosystem-specific operating knowledge;
- possible premium services such as audits, disaster-recovery exercises, migration, security review, or cloud optimization;
- a role in a trusted production operating model.

The DevOps provider receives a managed operations opportunity, not a guarantee of bank adoption, recurring revenue, exclusivity, or margin.

## Competitive Position

If the program succeeds, the DevOps provider improves its strategic position as a trusted managed operations partner for rural-bank digital transaction environments.

The intended positioning benefits include:

- repeatable deployments across similar bank environments;
- standardized operating practices;
- banking-grade operational discipline;
- stronger documentation and handover discipline;
- production reliability as a visible service;
- ecosystem-specific knowledge;
- readiness to support future ecosystems if the model expands;
- potential recognition as a qualified or approved provider if a certification model is later adopted.

This is strategic positioning, not a claim of exclusive provider status or certified approval. The provider's actual competitive outcome depends on reliability, documentation quality, response times, cost discipline, security practices, bank trust, ODTI coordination, and successful handovers.

## Commercial Delivery Models

The program should analyze three delivery models without concluding which is best.

### Model A: Direct Relationship

```text
Rural Bank
        |
        v
DevOps Provider
```

The rural bank contracts directly with the DevOps provider.

Advantages:

- strongest customer ownership;
- maximum transparency;
- easiest provider replacement;
- clear accountability between bank and provider.

Disadvantages and questions:

- ODTI coordination must be defined;
- support boundaries may require more documentation;
- ODTI has less control over provider performance;
- bank must be able to manage the provider relationship;
- commercial incentives may not align unless operating standards are clear.

Commercial implications:

- DevOps provider bills the rural bank directly;
- ODTI does not earn managed-cloud margin unless separately contracted;
- provider costs are transparent to the bank.

Governance implications:

- bank ownership is strongest;
- provider replacement is simplest;
- ODTI may need certification or operating standards to preserve ecosystem quality.

### Model B: ODTI Managed

```text
Rural Bank
        |
        v
ODTI
        |
        v
DevOps Provider
```

ODTI contracts with the DevOps provider.

Advantages:

- one commercial interface for the rural bank;
- simplified coordination;
- ODTI can align releases, support, and provider work;
- easier bundling into platform subscription or managed service proposals.

Disadvantages and questions:

- bank visibility into provider cost may be lower;
- ODTI may carry accountability for provider failures;
- margin must be explicit;
- provider replacement may be less transparent;
- vendor dependency concerns may increase if bank ownership is not protected.

Commercial implications:

- ODTI may resell managed operations;
- ODTI may earn markup if priced above provider cost;
- ODTI must track provider cost separately from platform revenue.

Governance implications:

- contracts must preserve bank ownership of production assets;
- ODTI must define support and escalation boundaries;
- provider handover procedures should still exist.

### Model C: Hybrid Approved Provider Model

ODTI certifies or approves one or more qualified DevOps providers. The rural bank chooses the provider, and the provider follows ecosystem standards.

Standards may cover:

- operational standards;
- deployment standards;
- backup standards;
- monitoring standards;
- security standards;
- release standards;
- documentation standards;
- handover standards.

Advantages:

- preserves bank choice;
- supports customer ownership;
- improves ecosystem quality through common standards;
- avoids unnecessary provider exclusivity;
- allows multiple qualified providers to participate;
- may reduce lock-in concerns.

Disadvantages and questions:

- certification governance must be defined;
- ODTI must decide whether it certifies, recommends, or merely documents standards;
- provider quality may vary;
- accountability across bank, ODTI, and provider must be clear;
- standards require maintenance.

Commercial implications:

- rural bank may contract directly with its chosen provider;
- ODTI may charge separately for platform services;
- certification or audit economics may emerge later but are not assumed;
- provider competition may pressure pricing and service quality.

Governance implications:

- this model may preserve customer choice and ecosystem trust;
- certification should not be implied before criteria exist;
- failed providers must be replaceable without transferring bank-owned assets.

This hybrid model deserves serious future consideration, but this document does not recommend it as the approved structure.

## If They Do Nothing

If the DevOps provider does not participate, it avoids immediate staffing, incident, security, and support risk.

The tradeoff is that the provider may miss a repeatable managed operations opportunity across rural-bank deployments and future ecosystems. The program may proceed with another provider, with ODTI-managed operations, or with bank-selected providers.

The "do nothing" case may be rational if service scope is unclear, monthly fees are underpriced, bank ownership is not protected, credential management is weak, incident liability is excessive, or staffing capacity is insufficient.
