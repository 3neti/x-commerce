# Participating Rural Bank

## Stakeholder

A participating rural bank is a member or eligible rural banking institution that may adopt selected digital transaction outcomes through the proposed RBAP Digital Banking Program.

This document is a stakeholder business-case draft. It is not evidence that any rural bank has accepted the program, approved pricing, agreed to contracts, or received legal or regulatory clearance.

## What's In It For Them?

The program is intended to enable participating rural banks to offer new branded digital financial services without replacing their existing core banking systems.

The strongest commercial idea is incremental modernization: the bank can start with the outcome most relevant to its market, such as payroll, remittance, government payouts, Pay Codes, merchant payments, collections, notifications, validation, or KYC-attached payouts. It does not need to adopt every capability at once.

If adoption is validated, each successful transaction can become a commercial event: a completed outcome that may strengthen the customer relationship, justify a fee, create value for a sponsor, attach a value-added service, or support a broader program relationship.

No revenue, adoption, customer retention, deposit growth, or regulatory outcome is guaranteed.

## Problem Today

Many rural banks face a practical modernization problem. Customers increasingly expect digital access, faster payments, remittances, notifications, and convenient transaction services, but full core banking replacement may be expensive, disruptive, and operationally risky.

The bank may also face constraints such as:

- limited internal engineering or DevOps capacity;
- fragmented vendor proposals;
- difficulty evaluating which digital service will actually earn or retain value;
- uncertainty over implementation and support obligations;
- customer pressure from larger banks, wallets, fintechs, remittance providers, and merchants;
- limited ability to package payroll, merchant, government, remittance, or collection relationships into repeatable digital services.

The program is designed to let the bank introduce useful transaction outcomes without making a single all-or-nothing technology decision.

## Why They Would Participate

A rural bank participates in the business case to evaluate whether the program can defend and deepen its local customer relationship.

Potential reasons include:

- offer branded digital services under the bank's own institutional presence;
- create additional fee-based services where approved;
- support employer payroll relationships;
- participate in domestic or inward remittance activity;
- support merchant payments and collections;
- compete for government benefit, subsidy, or payout programs;
- provide beneficiary notifications and transaction evidence;
- attach validation, KYC, feedback, surveys, or rider services to transactions;
- adopt digital services incrementally based on local demand;
- access managed operations options where internal capacity is limited.

These are commercial hypotheses. They must be tested through pricing, adoption, support load, provider costs, legal review, and operating evidence.

## Contribution

The program asks the rural bank to contribute:

- program adoption decision-making;
- license, subscription, or other approved commercial payments;
- onboarding participation;
- customer and market knowledge;
- product selection for the first launch use case;
- bank-side operations and support coordination;
- compliance, disclosure, and customer communication work;
- settlement or rail participation where applicable;
- promotion to relevant customers, employers, merchants, agencies, or sponsors;
- feedback on usage, support issues, and commercial viability.

The bank should not be expected to contribute undefined operational obligations. Support, cloud, settlement, customer service, compliance, and incident responsibilities must be documented before launch.

## Receives

Depending on the approved model, the program is intended to provide the rural bank with:

- access to a rural-bank-branded digital transaction platform;
- selected transaction outcomes such as payroll, remittance, payouts, collections, merchant payments, or Pay Codes;
- onboarding and training;
- reporting;
- transaction evidence;
- value-added service menu options;
- managed cloud or operations options;
- commercial documentation for stakeholder discussion;
- a path to expand from one use case to additional services.

The bank receives commercial capability, not a guarantee of customer adoption.

## Competitive Position

If the program succeeds, the participating rural bank improves its strategic position by remaining the trusted local financial institution while expanding the range of digital services it can offer under its own brand.

The intended positioning benefits include:

- preserving the bank's local customer relationship as more transactions become digital;
- offering branded digital services without replacing the existing core banking system;
- competing more credibly with larger banks, wallets, fintechs, remittance providers, and other digital channels;
- introducing new capabilities incrementally instead of through a single transformation project;
- deepening relationships with employers, merchants, remittance partners, agencies, and program sponsors;
- remaining locally relevant while building digital transaction capability;
- creating a practical path from one successful use case to a broader service menu.

This is strategic positioning, not a claim of market leadership. The bank's actual competitive outcome depends on adoption, operating performance, customer trust, pricing, legal readiness, and the quality of the first use case.

## Revenue Or Value Drivers

Possible value drivers include:

- bank-retained transaction fees;
- service fees approved for specific outcomes;
- payroll account or relationship value;
- remittance volume and customer engagement;
- merchant payment or collection relationships;
- government program participation;
- value-added service markup or revenue share, where approved;
- customer retention;
- lower opportunity cost compared with full replacement;
- stronger local brand relevance.

The model must separate:

- gross transaction value;
- pass-through funds;
- customer deposits or settlement balances;
- bank-retained service fees;
- ODTI platform or transaction charges;
- NetBank rail or settlement charges;
- value-added provider costs;
- taxes;
- 3neti royalties where applicable;
- partner commissions where approved.

Pass-through funds, settlement balances, and customer deposits must not be treated as bank revenue merely because they move through or relate to the program.

## Costs

Potential bank costs include:

- license, subscription, maintenance, or activation fees;
- implementation and onboarding costs;
- cloud setup or recurring managed operations fees;
- bank-side staff time;
- training;
- customer support;
- marketing and adoption campaigns;
- compliance and legal review;
- settlement or rail fees;
- value-added service charges;
- dispute handling;
- reporting and audit support;
- contingency for support or integration complexity.

All costs should be modeled by scenario before the bank is asked to treat the program as financially attractive.

## Risks

Key risks for the rural bank include:

- customer adoption is lower than expected;
- transaction volume does not cover fixed program costs;
- support burden exceeds planned capacity;
- the first use case is poorly matched to local demand;
- value-added provider costs reduce margin;
- cloud or DevOps responsibilities are unclear;
- settlement, rail, or provider outages affect customer experience;
- legal terminology is misunderstood by customers;
- regulatory characterization requires changes to product language or workflow;
- the bank is seen as endorsing services before contracts, disclosures, and support boundaries are ready.

The bank should not rely on optimistic projections without sensitivity analysis.

## Responsibilities

The bank may own or share responsibility for:

- choosing the first use case;
- approving customer-facing language;
- customer onboarding and education;
- first-line customer support, if assigned;
- compliance review and disclosures;
- fee approval and customer communication;
- settlement account or rail arrangements where applicable;
- bank-side reconciliation;
- escalation of incidents and disputes;
- local marketing and adoption;
- feedback to ODTI, RBAP, providers, and implementation teams.

Responsibilities should be stated in contracts, onboarding plans, support documents, and service-level models.

## Five-Year Outcome

The five-year model should not begin with a revenue promise. It should begin with assumptions.

Minimum bank-side inputs:

- onboarding or activation cost;
- annual license, subscription, or maintenance cost;
- managed cloud setup and recurring fees;
- first launch use case;
- banks' target customer segment;
- active customers or sponsors;
- transactions per month;
- transaction growth;
- bank-retained fee per transaction;
- platform charge paid to ODTI;
- settlement or rail charges;
- value-added service adoption rates;
- value-added provider costs;
- support cost per transaction or per active customer;
- churn;
- refunds, disputes, bad debt, or failed transactions;
- taxes and contingency.

Required scenarios:

- Conservative: slow adoption, limited value-added service usage, higher support cost.
- Base: moderate adoption, practical onboarding, selective value-added services.
- Accelerated: stronger adoption, repeatable operations, broader product expansion.

The model should show payback period, net contribution, support burden, and sensitivity to transaction volume. It should also show what happens if the bank adopts only one module at first.

## If They Do Nothing

If the rural bank does nothing, it may avoid immediate license, implementation, and operating costs.

The tradeoff is that digital service gaps may persist. Customers, employers, merchants, remitters, and program sponsors may continue shifting activity to larger banks, wallets, fintechs, remittance providers, or informal alternatives. The bank may lose opportunities to build payroll, merchant, remittance, and government-program relationships through its own brand.

The "do nothing" case should still be modeled honestly. Avoiding cost may be rational if the bank's target market, support capacity, legal readiness, or expected transaction volume is not yet sufficient.

## Open Decisions

- Which rural-bank segment is the first target?
- Which first use case should be offered to the bank?
- Which license model applies: perpetual plus maintenance, annual subscription, or hybrid?
- Which fees are paid by the bank, sponsor, customer, merchant, employer, or agency?
- What revenue does the bank retain?
- Which platform or transaction charge does ODTI receive?
- Which NetBank fees apply, if any?
- Which value-added services are offered at launch?
- Which managed cloud model applies?
- Who provides first-line customer support?
- What legal terminology may be used externally?
- What disclosures, contracts, and approvals are required before launch?
